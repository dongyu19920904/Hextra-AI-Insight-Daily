## **🔥 Today's Top 10 Highlights**

### 1. DeepSeek's Open-Source Inference Framework Matters More Than Open-Source Models

**The inference framework is now open source.** As described in [this WeChat analysis article](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/5MEu2eeqNfBWXlkCKTd6sg), DeepSeek has made its inference framework DeepInfer public. The technical highlights are **MoE expert parallelism** and speculative decoding, allowing the same compute to serve more concurrent requests. Open-sourcing the model solves "who can use it"; the inference framework solves **"whether the cost actually works out."** Developers currently running batch inference jobs can reassess their deployment costs today.

### 8. An AI Video Recognition System Writes Its Own Code to Compensate for Visual Weaknesses

### 9. WhatsApp Help Pages Contained Hidden Instructions Limiting Its Customer Service AI

**All hidden instructions have since been removed.** [The Redact author examined 545 pages](https://redact.dev/blog/whatsapp-help-pages-hidden-ai-instructions) and found **102 AI text blocks** across **80 pages** that were not visible to users. Teams building customer service AI need to draw a clear line between internal instructions and public-facing user rights disclosures.

### 10. US DHS Formally Orders Federal Agencies to Remove DeepSeek

**The federal agency ban is now official.** According to [the US DHS formally ordering federal agencies to remove DeepSeek](https://www.reuters.com/technology/us-banning-chinese-ai-deepseek-government-agency-it-systems-2026-10-19/), the US Department of Homeland Security issued a notice on **October 19, 2026** requiring US civilian government agencies to remove DeepSeek products and related third-party services from their IT systems. DHS cited **"multiple threat vectors"** posed by DeepSeek, with concerns that sensitive data could be transferred to China. Multiple federal agencies and state governments had already taken similar steps beforehand.
